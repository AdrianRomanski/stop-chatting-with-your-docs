# The first three <span class="hot">mistakes</span>

<div class="kt-center" style="margin-top: 18px">
  <FirstMistakes />
</div>

<!--
⏱ 1:05 · script: [STAGE FirstMistakes "before the first note"] The first three *mistakes* ⏱ 65s

Three mistakes almost everybody makes when they start a second brain. I made all three.

Mistake one: over-structuring. Before the first note even exists, we build the perfect tree. Work, ideas, books, misc, and under each of them five more folders, for every project we might have some day. (folders pop) Hundreds of empty directories. And the first note? It's still hovering up there, with no idea where to go. (note bobs)

Forte has a line for that, in chapter 5: the goal of organizing is to move your goals forward, "not get a PhD in notetaking." Move quickly, touch lightly.

Mistake two: holding it all in your head. (head strains) Every idea, every todo, every "I'll remember that". It doesn't make us stronger. It makes us weaker: all the energy goes into holding, none into thinking.

Forte opens chapter one with David Allen, the Getting Things Done guy: "Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them."

So we make space. We move the notes out, into a second brain. (arm grows, notes fly into the vault)

And here's the part I love. Forte has a footnote in chapter 2: when you keep using a tool to extend your reach, your brain's map of your body grows to include it. So that vault isn't a drawer next to you. It's a new limb. Part of your body. (everything lights up the same color) It doesn't narrow what you can do. It expands it.

Mistake three: overthinking the note. (the note types and erases itself) We read something good, and before we save it we start an exam. Is it useful? Is it logical? Is it complete? We write a line, delete it, write it again.

But there's no right answer here. What goes into a note is subjective. It has to resonate with you. Forte, chapter 3: "Don't make it an analytical decision." Just look inside for a feeling of pleasure, curiosity, wonder or excitement, "and let that be your signal." (the heart beats, the note writes itself)

Emotion and intuition, not logic. If it resonates, keep it.
-->

# File by <span class="hot">actionability</span>, not topic

<div class="kt-center" style="margin-top: 22px">
  <OrganizeFlow />
</div>

<!--
⏱ 0:55 · script: [STAGE OrganizeFlow "O · Organize with PARA"] File by *actionability*, not topic ⏱ 55s

Organize. Remember the train? The idea that lit up: I need an offline sparring partner. (the bulb) I didn't build anything that night. I wrote it into my daily note. (the thought becomes the note)

Now, where does it go? Most of us file like a library: Dewey decimal, this goes under Psychology. (shelf, struck out) Forte tried that himself, "even the Dewey decimal system used in libraries", and "every method I tried soon failed."

His fix is PARA, and it's not a set of folders, it's a flow. Every note answers the same questions, in this order: project, area, resource, and if none of them, archive. (the flow comes in, folders empty)

My sparring partner. Is it for a project, something with a deadline? (the note travels) Yes. This talk. (green)

Next note in the same daily note: "I want to find my voice on stage and mature as a speaker." Project? No, it never ends. Area? Yes. Public speaking, a standard I keep up. (green)

And the books I read for this talk: Building a Second Brain, Make It Stick, Richard Feynman's Mental Models, The Socratic Method, Tomorrowmind. Project? No. Area? No. Resource? Yes. (books land)

None of those? Archive it. My last talk lives there: that project is finished. (daily note: all filed)

Forte's one question: "In which project will this be most useful?" Not where it came from. Where it's going.
-->

# One note, four <span class="hot">layers</span>

<div class="kt-center" style="margin-top: 22px">
  <DistillLayers />
</div>

<!--
⏱ 0:30 · script: [STAGE DistillLayers "D · Distill"] One note, four *layers* ⏱ 30s

Distill. Forte calls it progressive summarization, and it happens when a project needs the note, not on day one.

Layer one: the raw passage I clipped. (raw)
Layer two: I bold what matters. (bold)
Layer three: I highlight the best of the bold. Everything else steps back. (highlight)
Layer four, on top: a summary in my own words. (summary) "Feeling over logic. Keep what resonates."

Every time I come back, I go one layer deeper, and the next time my eyes find the essence in two seconds.
-->

<h1 class="s">“We only know<br>what we <span class="hot">make</span>”</h1>

<div class="kt-quote-by">— Giambattista Vico</div>

<div class="kt-center" style="margin-top: 18px">
  <ConsumeCreate />
</div>

<!--
⏱ 0:25 · script: [STAGE ConsumeCreate "from consuming to creating"] “We only know / what we *make*” ⏱ 25s

Forte's whole book points one way. Chapter 3: "shift as much of your time and effort as possible from consuming to creating."

He splits CODE in two halves. Capture and Organize are divergence: we open up, collect, take things in. (inputs flow into the brain) Distill and Express are convergence: we narrow down, decide, make something. (the brain lights up, the whiteboard draws itself)

And in chapter 7 he borrows a line from the philosopher Giambattista Vico: "We only know what we make." (pause on the quote)

Not what we read. Not what an AI summarized for us. What we make. And convergence is exactly the half we're tempted to hand to an LLM: let it synthesize, let it draft, and our own brain never fires.
-->
